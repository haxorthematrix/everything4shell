    Naming rules (mandatory, unenforceable)
    
    
      
      
    1. Four syllables maximum. It has to survive being said out loud on a conference stage by someone who is nervous.
      2. 
      
    2. The domain comes first. Register before you report. A vulnerability without a landing page is just a ticket.
      3. 
      
    3. The logo precedes the patch. If there is no logo, journalists will invent one, and it will be a padlock.
      4. 
      
    4. Never name it after the vendor. Name it after the ecosystem, so that everyone has to care.
      5. 
      
    5. Severity is a marketing field. CVSS caps at 10, which is a failure of imagination.
      6. 
      
    6. One bug, one name. Three bugs, also one name. Nine bugs, one name and a keynote.
      7.

    A short history of the movement
    
    
    Twelve years from “we should give this one a name” to “we should give this one a brand book.”
    
  
    
  
      
    
    1. 2014Heartbleed ships with a logo and a website. Security communication is never the same. This was, to be fair, a genuinely good idea.
      2. 
    
    2. 2014Shellshock establishes that “shell” is the most alarming word in English.
      3. 
    
    3. 2017EternalBlue proves a name can outlive its vendor’s patch cycle by several years.
      4. 
    
    4. 2018Spectre and Meltdown arrive with two logos and a performance regression.
      5. 
    
    5. 2021Log4Shell. A logging library becomes a holiday-season cancellation event. The -4Shell suffix is born.
      6. 
    
    6. 2022Spring4Shell and Text4Shell arrive within months. The suffix is now a template.
      7. 
    
    7. 2024regreSSHion demonstrates that puns are also acceptable.
      8. 
    
    8. 2025Naming velocity exceeds patching velocity. Several vulnerabilities are now named twice.
      9. 
    
    9. 2026The Shell Naming Authority publishes Everything4Shell, a single name covering all current and future vulnerabilities. Disclosure is now O(1).
      10.

    FAQ
    
  
    
    
    Is this a real vulnerability?
    No. Everything4Shell is satire. There is no CVE, no exploit, no advisory, and no patch. If a scanner reports Everything4Shell, the scanner is also satire.
    
    
    Why 4 and not for?
    Because Log4j contained a 4, and the entire industry pattern-matched on it rather than on the vulnerability class. This is the central joke and also, unfortunately, the central truth.
    
    
    Can I use these names for a real vulnerability?
    Yes. Royalty-free, no attribution required. If your name comes from the generator, please at least verify the bug exists first. That is the one step the industry keeps skipping.
    
    
    Are named vulnerabilities actually bad?
    No — and this is worth saying plainly. Heartbleed got patched fast because it had a name, a logo and a website that a CFO could understand. Naming is a genuinely effective communication tool. The failure mode is when the brand arrives before the analysis, when severity gets inflated for reach, and when three unrelated bugs get merged into one scary word because the word is what gets clicks.
    
    
    Is my grandmother affected?
    Yes. Her router is affected, her car is affected, and the loyalty card in her wallet is affected. She is handling it better than your incident channel.
    
    
    Who runs the Shell Naming Authority?
    It is one person, a domain renewal reminder, and this repository. The SNA has no members, no charter, and no standing. Like several real standards bodies, it has a logo.
    
    
    How do I report a vulnerability in this website?
    Open an issue. Name it first.
